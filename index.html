⟦ 4. Kobe Port / Meriken Park Walk — 1–2 hrs (Walk) ⟧

Things to see
Kobe Harbor
  • Meriken Park — Waterfront park built on reclaimed port land and opened in 1987 as part of Kobe’s modern harbor redevelopment. The area preserves Kobe’s identity as one of Japan’s major international ports after its 1868 opening to foreign trade.
  • Kobe Port Tower — Kobe’s red hyperboloid observation tower opened in 1963 and became one of the city’s strongest skyline symbols. Its form was designed to evoke a Japanese tsuzumi hand drum.
  • BE KOBE Monument — A post-earthquake civic monument introduced after the 1995 Great Hanshin-Awaji Earthquake recovery era. It represents Kobe’s rebuilt waterfront identity and is now one of the harbor’s signature photo landmarks.
  • Harborland — Large shopping and entertainment district developed on the west side of Kobe’s port in the 1990s. It ties the historic harbor edge to the modern city waterfront.

⟦ 9. Dotonbori Night Walk + Donki / Ebisu — 60–90 min (Walk / Activity) ⟧

Things to see
Dotonbori
  • Dotonbori River Walk — The Dōtonbori canal was completed around 1615, and the modern riverside promenade developed through later twentieth-century waterfront improvements. The canal district grew around entertainment theaters during the Edo period and became Osaka’s best-known nightlife corridor. Its neon, food signs, and bridge views define the modern Dotonbori image.
  • Glico Sign — The running-man sign has advertised Ezaki Glico above Dotonbori since 1935, with the current version continuing one of Osaka’s most recognizable commercial landmarks. It is closely associated with Ebisubashi Bridge and the city’s food-and-neon culture.
Activities
Dotonbori
  • Don Quijote Dotonbori Store Run — Don Quijote developed from a discount business founded in 1980, with the first Don Quijote-branded store opening in 1989. Browsing the densely packed Dotonbori branch provides a direct experience of Japan’s maximalist discount-store culture and its unusual mix of practical goods, souvenirs, food, cosmetics, and novelty products.
  • Ebisu Night Ride — The Ebisu Tower Ferris wheel opened in 2005 as part of the Don Quijote Dotonbori building and was designed as an unusual vertical oval wheel integrated into the storefront. Riding it turns the district’s neon signs, canal, and crowds into an elevated introduction to Osaka nightlife.

⟦ 14. Osaka Castle Iso Adventure — 2–2.5 hrs (Walk / Activity) ⟧

Things to see
Osaka
  • Ōte-mon Gate— The original western entrance belonged to Toyotomi Hideyoshi’s Osaka Castle of the 1580s; the existing Ōte-mon Gate was built in 1628 during the Tokugawa reconstruction. Its massive timber-and-iron design demonstrates the controlled ceremonial and defensive approach to the castle.
  • Nishinomaru Garden — The present public garden opened around 1965 within the former western bailey of Osaka Castle. Former western bailey area of Osaka Castle, now a paid garden known for wide lawns and cherry blossoms. It gives one of the clearest exterior views of the main tower across the moat and stone walls.
  • Osaka Castle — Toyotomi Hideyoshi began the original castle in 1583 as a symbol of national unification power. The current main tower dates to 1931, but the site remains tied to the Toyotomi regime and the Siege of Osaka in 1614–1615.
  • Hokoku Shrine — Hokoku Shrine was founded in 1879, and its present Osaka Castle Park complex dates largely to its relocation in 1961. Shrine honoring Toyotomi Hideyoshi, the warlord most strongly associated with Osaka Castle’s rise. Its presence inside the park keeps the castle’s Toyotomi legacy visible beyond the main tower.
  • Osaka Castle Park Fountain — The fountain likely dates to the postwar redevelopment of Osaka Castle Park, roughly the 1960s–1980s. East-side park landmark within the broad public grounds surrounding Osaka Castle. It marks the transition from the central castle zone into the modern park and event areas.
  • Cool Japan Park Osaka WW Hall — Modern theater complex opened in 2019 as part of Osaka’s entertainment and cultural-event infrastructure. Its placement beside the castle park shows how the historic grounds now function as both heritage space and city venue.
  • Forest of Memorial Trees — The memorial woodland likely dates to postwar park landscaping, roughly the 1960s–1980s. Quiet wooded section of Osaka Castle Park with a more reflective atmosphere than the main tourist path. It adds a softer memorial landscape between the theater area and open plazas.
  • Sun Square — Sun Square was likely laid out during late-twentieth-century park redevelopment, roughly the 1970s–1990s. Open plaza area on the east side of Osaka Castle Park used as a circulation and gathering space. It reflects the park’s modern role as a public recreation zone around the historic castle core.
  • Baseball field — The baseball field likely dates to Osaka Castle Park’s postwar conversion into a major public recreation area, roughly the 1950s–1970s. Athletic field within Osaka Castle Park, showing how the former military and castle grounds were converted into public urban parkland. It is a reminder that the area functions as a living city park, not only a preserved monument.
  • Artillery Arsenal area — The Osaka Army Arsenal was established in this area around 1870 and expanded through the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Osaka Army Arsenal operated in this wider area from the Meiji period until World War II and was one of Japan’s major military production sites. Its remnants and memory add a modern wartime layer to the older castle landscape.
  • Plum Grove — The present public Plum Grove was developed around the 1970s as part of Osaka Castle Park’s modern landscaping. Seasonal grove inside Osaka Castle Park known for late-winter and early-spring plum blossoms. It adds a classical garden element to the fortress landscape before the cherry blossom season peaks.
  • Aoyamon Gate— A northeast gate approach existed in the Toyotomi castle of the late 1500s, while the existing Aoyamon Gate was built during the Tokugawa reconstruction in the 1620s. It marks the transition from the Plum Grove and outer park back toward the fortified inner castle area.
  • Gokuraku-bashi Bridge— The original wooden Gokuraku-bashi was built around 1626 during the Tokugawa reconstruction of Osaka Castle; after it was lost in 1868, the present concrete bridge was reconstructed in 1965. It crosses the inner moat toward the central enclosure and provides one of the castle’s strongest moat-and-stone-wall views.
  • Site of Haunted House — The present marker appears to be a late-twentieth- or early-twenty-first-century local map feature tied to older folklore. It is a small local curiosity within Osaka Castle Park rather than a major historic monument, adding an unusual layer of folklore and map history to the castle grounds.

⟦ 18. Nara Park Iso Adventure — 2.5–3 hrs (Walk / Activity) ⟧

Things to see
Nara
  • Tokon-do Temple — Eastern Golden Hall of Kōfuku-ji, originally established in 726 during the Nara period. The hall links the walk to the powerful Fujiwara clan temple complex that shaped early Nara Buddhism.
  • Kasuga Pagoda Ruins — The pagoda complex developed between roughly the ninth and twelfth centuries within the Kasuga–Kōfuku-ji sacred landscape. Remains tied to the larger Kasuga/Kōfuku-ji sacred landscape on the edge of Nara Park. The ruins hint at how dense the old temple-shrine zone once was around the Fujiwara institutions.
  • Nara National Museum — National museum opened in 1895 and best known for Buddhist art, sculpture, and ritual objects. Its location beside Nara Park places it directly inside the old capital’s temple-and-shrine corridor.
  • Meiji Emperor’s Throne Site — The site and its commemorative treatment likely date to the late nineteenth century, roughly the 1870s–1890s. Historical marker connected to imperial presence in Nara during the modern Meiji period. It adds a nineteenth-century imperial layer to an area better known for Nara-period Buddhism.
  • Deer Park — Central deer area of Nara Park, where the deer are traditionally regarded as messengers of the Kasuga deity. Nara Park was formally established in 1880, but the sacred-deer tradition is much older.
  • Sacred Deer Statue — The present deer monument appears to date to the mid- or late twentieth century. Modern marker honoring the deer’s religious and cultural role in Nara. It reflects the connection between the park’s famous deer and Kasuga Taisha’s shrine tradition.
  • Second Torii Gate — The present gate likely dates to an Edo-period rebuilding, roughly the seventeenth century. Gate on the approach into the Kasuga Taisha side of Nara Park. It signals the shift from open deer park into the shrine’s more sacred wooded approach.
  • Honsha Osugi / 本社大杉 (Main Shrine Great Cedar) — The cedar is commonly understood to be many centuries old and likely began growing roughly between the eleventh and thirteenth centuries. Great cedar associated with the Kasuga Taisha main precinct. Large old trees are part of the shrine’s sacred forest atmosphere and help define the approach to the main shrine.
  • Kasuga Taisha main precinct area — Kasuga Taisha was founded in 768 as the tutelary shrine of the Fujiwara clan. The shrine is famous for its lanterns, sacred forest, and role in Nara’s UNESCO-listed historic landscape.
  • Mizuya Shrine — Mizuya Shrine likely developed within Kasuga Taisha’s medieval subsidiary-shrine network, roughly between the twelfth and fifteenth centuries. Small shrine within the Kasuga Taisha sacred precinct. It adds one of the many subsidiary-shrine layers that make the approach feel dense and ritual-focused.
  • Sansha Takusen Pond — The pond likely formed part of the shrine landscape by the Edo period, roughly between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries. Pond waypoint within the shrine-to-Tōdai-ji walking zone. It provides a quieter water feature between the Kasuga side and the large Tōdai-ji gate area.
  • Nandaimon Gate— Tōdai-ji’s original Great South Gate was built in the eighth century and was destroyed by a typhoon in the tenth century; the existing gate was raised beginning in 1199 and completed in 1203. Its immense timber frame and monumental guardian statues represent the scale of the medieval reconstruction of Tōdai-ji.
  • Nekodan Cat Stairway — The present stairway and its local name likely date to the twentieth century. The small named stairway is a local curiosity within the Tōdai-ji area and contributes to the dense network of minor paths and landmarks surrounding the major temple buildings.
  • Todai-ji Nagaike — Nagaike likely developed as part of the temple landscape between the medieval and early-modern periods, roughly the twelfth–seventeenth centuries. The pond contributes a quieter landscape element to the temple precinct and reflects the importance of water, seasonal scenery, and visual calm within Japanese religious grounds.
  • Great Ginkgo Tree — The tree was likely planted several centuries ago, roughly between the sixteenth and eighteenth centuries. Its size and seasonal yellow foliage make it a living landmark, while ginkgo trees are commonly valued around temples and shrines for longevity, resilience, and dramatic autumn color.
  • Todaiji Temple Kaidan Hall — The ordination hall was founded in 754, while the present main structure dates largely to an early-eighteenth-century rebuilding around 1733. Ordination-hall area connected to Tōdai-ji’s role as one of ancient Japan’s most important Buddhist institutions. Tōdai-ji was founded in the eighth century and became the head temple of state Buddhism in Nara.
Activities
Nara
  • Feed the Deer — Nara Park was formally established in 1880, while the belief that its deer are sacred messengers of the Kasuga deities dates back many centuries. Buying shika senbei and feeding the free-roaming deer participates in Nara’s defining visitor tradition and gives direct context to the city’s shrine culture.

⟦ 30. Uji River / Byodo-in / Daikichiyama Iso Adventure — 2.25–3.25 hrs (Walk / Activity) ⟧

Things to see
Uji
  • Statue of Murasaki Shikibu — The present statue appears to have been installed in the late 1990s or early 2000s. It honors the Heian-period author of The Tale of Genji and emphasizes Uji’s literary importance as the setting of the novel’s final Uji Chapters.
  • Byodo-in Omotesando / Uji Tea Street — The approach street developed with Uji’s tea trade from roughly the fourteenth century onward, while its present commercial streetscape is largely twentieth century. The temple approach developed around Uji’s long tea-trading tradition, which expanded from the medieval period after tea cultivation reached the region. Walking the shop-lined street connects Byodo-in with Uji’s identity as one of Japan’s most important tea centers.
  • Byodo-in Omotemon / Front Gate — Main entry gate to Byodo-in, a temple founded in 1052 when Fujiwara no Yorimichi converted a noble villa into a Buddhist temple. The site is most famous for Phoenix Hall, completed in 1053 and later depicted on the Japanese 10-yen coin.
  • Byodo-in Nammon / South Gate — The present South Gate likely dates to an Edo-period rebuilding, roughly the seventeenth or eighteenth century. It forms one of the formal boundaries of the temple precinct and preserves the architectural idea of entering and leaving sacred space through clearly defined gates.
  • Kisen Bridge — The present Kisen Bridge likely dates to late-twentieth-century riverfront development, roughly the 1970s–1990s. It connects the riverbank with the Uji River islands and contributes to a landscape long associated with poetry, pilgrimage, and the literary atmosphere of The Tale of Genji.
  • Asagiri Bridge — The present Asagiri Bridge dates to approximately the mid-1990s, around 1995. Linking the river islands with Uji’s east bank, it frames broad views of the water and reinforces the area’s association with Heian-period literature and scenic river culture.
  • Uji Shrine — Uji Shrine has traditional origins in roughly the fourth or fifth century, while its surviving buildings are largely medieval or early modern. The shrine is associated with Prince Uji no Wakiiratsuko and preserves an important layer of early imperial legend and local worship on the east bank of the Uji River.
  • Ujigami Shrine — Ujigami Shrine’s surviving main hall dates to roughly the late eleventh century, around the 1060s. The present main sanctuary dates to the late Heian period and is often identified as Japan’s oldest surviving original Shinto shrine building. Its UNESCO-listed architecture is important for showing early shrine design and the sacred history of Uji beyond Byodo-in.
  • Daikichiyama Observation Deck — The observation deck likely dates to late-twentieth-century park development, roughly the 1970s–1990s. The elevated viewpoint overlooks the Uji River, Byodo-in, and the surrounding city, making the geography of the literary and religious landscape visible from above.

⟦ 34. Fushimi Inari Iso Adventure — 85–100 min (Walk / Activity) ⟧

Things to see
Fushimi-Inari
  • Great Torii — The present large entrance torii likely dates to an early- or mid-twentieth-century rebuilding. Large entrance torii marking the formal approach into Fushimi Inari Taisha. It frames the transition from the station-side street into the shrine’s sacred lower precinct.
  • Fushimi Inari Shrine / Second Torii approach — Lower approach area leading into one of Kyoto’s most important Inari shrines. Fushimi Inari is traditionally dated to 711 and is dedicated to Inari, a deity associated with rice, prosperity, and business.
  • Senbon Torii — The custom of donating torii at Fushimi Inari became widespread by the Edo period, beginning roughly in the seventeenth century, while individual gates are continually replaced. Dense corridor of donated vermilion torii gates and the shrine’s most famous visual feature. The gates are offerings from individuals and businesses, turning the mountain path into a layered record of devotion and sponsorship.
